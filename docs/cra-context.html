CRA Context

CRANE is structured around the Cyber Resilience Act itself. This page maps the regulation's key building blocks — scope, essential requirements, documentation and reporting — to where they live in the tool.

Not legal advice

CRANE helps you organise and evidence your compliance work; it does not interpret the law for you or replace qualified legal or conformity-assessment advice. Always confirm obligations against the official regulation text and your Notified Body where applicable.

What the CRA covers

The Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) sets binding cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements placed on the EU market. It applies across the whole product lifecycle — from design and the components you ship, through vulnerability handling and security updates, to the support period you commit to. Manufacturers carry the bulk of the obligations, with related duties for importers and distributors.

The CRA is horizontal — it applies across sectors and product types, from consumer IoT to industrial control systems, unless a sector-specific regulation already provides equivalent requirements (such as certain medical devices under MDR).

Article 3 — scope & definitions

Article 3 defines what counts as a product with digital elements. The key test: does the product have components that process data, and does that processing have a direct or indirect connection to a network or device? If yes, the CRA almost certainly applies.

What is in scope

  • Hardware products with digital elements (connected sensors, routers, cameras, smart appliances)
  • Software products placed on the market as standalone products (desktop applications, mobile apps, firmware)
  • Remote data processing solutions integral to the product's function (Article 3(2))

What is out of scope

  • Products already covered by sector-specific EU regulation with equivalent requirements (certain medical devices, vehicles, aviation)
  • Products developed or modified exclusively for national security or military purposes
  • Pure SaaS services with no installable component (though cloud backends of in-scope products may be covered via Article 3(2))
  • Open source software developed entirely outside a commercial context

Remote data processing solutions (Article 3(2))

Article 3(2) extends the product scope to cover cloud backends and remote services whose absence would prevent the product from performing its intended function. CRANE includes a step-by-step classification wizard to help you assess whether a remote processing element is in scope and document the rationale for that decision.

The Article 3(2) wizard produces a documented, repeatable scope decision — useful when a consultant or assessor later asks why a backend was or wasn't treated as in scope.

Product classification

The CRA introduces a risk-based classification system. Most products are default class — but products that perform security-relevant functions may fall into Important Class I or Important Class II, which require stricter conformity assessment routes.

DefaultGeneral-purpose software, connected toys, smart speakersManufacturer self-assessment
Important — Class IIdentity management, browsers, password managers, VPNs, network switchesHarmonised standard + self-assessment, or third-party
Important — Class IIHypervisors, PKI, industrial automation, safety-critical devices, firewallsThird-party Notified Body mandatory

Annex I — essential requirements

Annex I sets out the essential cybersecurity requirements in two parts:

Part I — Product security properties covers what the product itself must achieve: protection from unauthorised access, data confidentiality and integrity, minimisation of attack surface, resilience against denial of service, security by default configuration, and the ability to receive security updates.

Part II — Vulnerability handling requirements covers what the manufacturer must do: maintain and publish a software bill of materials, provide security updates without undue delay, operate a coordinated disclosure policy, notify authorities of actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents, and define a support period.

CRANE's Annex I matrix turns this into a living coverage map: each requirement is tracked per product, with its status and links to the evidence that satisfies it.

Annex II — technical documentation

Annex II describes the technical documentation a manufacturer must compile to demonstrate conformity. It includes a general description of the product, design and development documentation including risk assessments, information on security measures implemented, a software bill of materials, the vulnerability handling policy and processes, and a list of harmonised standards applied.

Because CRANE already holds the underlying evidence, it can assemble a technical documentation export in one step, ready for internal conformity assessment and CE marking.

Vulnerability & incident reporting (Article 14)

The CRA introduces strict timelines for reporting to ENISA and relevant national authorities:

EventDeadline
Early warning of actively exploited vulnerability24 hours after becoming aware
Vulnerability notification72 hours after becoming aware
Final vulnerability report14 days after fix is available
Severe incident early warning24 hours after becoming aware
Severe incident notification72 hours after becoming aware

CRANE's vulnerability records, advisories and audit trail give you the structured, timestamped basis these reports require. Full Article 14 reporting support is on CRANE's near-term roadmap, targeting the 11 September 2026 deadline.

Substantial changes (Article 3(4))

When a product changes in a way that affects its compliance, the CRA may treat it as a substantial change — potentially triggering a fresh conformity assessment as if it were a new product. CRANE lets you record changes, assess substantiality with documented rationale, and track re-conformity work triggered by that decision.

Compliance timeline

  1. Regulation in force — November 2024

    The regulation entered into force. The transition period begins. Manufacturers should start compliance programmes now — evidence collection is most valuable when it covers the product's whole lifecycle.

  2. Reporting obligations — 11 September 2026

    Duties around reporting actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents begin to apply (Article 14). CRANE's near-term roadmap targets full support for these reporting workflows by this date.

  3. Main obligations apply — 11 December 2027

    The full set of manufacturer obligations applies: essential requirements, conformity assessment, CE marking, technical documentation, and the declaration of conformity.

Starting evidence collection early matters — the audit trail and version history are most valuable when they cover a product's whole life, not just the weeks before an assessment deadline.
