CRA Context

CRANE is structured around the Cyber Resilience Act itself. This page maps the regulation's key building blocks — scope, essential requirements, documentation and reporting — to where they live in the tool.

Not legal advice

CRANE helps you organise and evidence your compliance work; it does not interpret the law for you or replace qualified legal or conformity-assessment advice. Always confirm obligations against the official regulation and your Notified Body where applicable.

What the CRA covers

The Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) sets binding cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements placed on the EU market. It applies across the lifecycle — from design and the components you ship, through vulnerability handling, to the support period you commit to. Manufacturers carry the bulk of the obligations, with related duties for importers and distributors.

Article 3 — scope & definitions

Article 3 defines what counts as a product with digital elements and introduces the concept of remote data processing solutions (Article 3(2)) — cloud backends and update servers whose absence would prevent the product from performing its function. These are pulled into scope alongside the product itself.

CRANE includes a classification wizard built on the DIGITALEUROPE I1 / I3 / I5 / I6 guidance to help you assess whether a remote processing element is in scope, and to document the rationale for that decision.

The Article 3(2) wizard produces a documented, repeatable scope decision — useful when a consultant or assessor later asks why a backend was or wasn't treated as in scope.

Annex I — essential requirements

Annex I sets out the essential cybersecurity requirements, in two parts: Part I covers product properties (secure by default, protection of data, attack-surface minimisation, and so on); Part II covers vulnerability-handling requirements (an SBOM, timely security updates, a coordinated disclosure policy, and more).

CRANE's Annex I matrix turns this into a living coverage map: each requirement is tracked per product, with its status and links to the evidence that satisfies it.

Annex II — technical documentation

Annex II describes the technical documentation a manufacturer must compile to demonstrate conformity. Because CRANE already holds the underlying evidence — SBOMs, assessments, vulnerability records, the Annex I coverage map — it can assemble a technical documentation export in one step, ready for internal conformity assessment and CE marking.

Vulnerability & incident reporting

The CRA introduces duties to report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents to the relevant authorities within tight deadlines. CRANE's vulnerability records, advisories and audit trail give you the structured, timestamped basis these reports require.

Substantial changes

When a product changes in a way that affects its compliance, the CRA may treat it as a substantial change — potentially triggering a fresh conformity assessment. CRANE lets you assess a change and track the re-conformity work it creates, with the version history to show what changed and when.

Compliance timeline

The CRA's obligations phase in over time. The exact dates and their interpretation should always be confirmed against the official text, but the broad shape is:

  1. Entry into force

    The regulation is in force; the transition period begins.

  2. Reporting obligations — 11 September 2026

    Duties around reporting actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents begin to apply. CRANE's near-term roadmap targets full support for these reporting obligations by this date.

  3. Main obligations apply

    The full set of manufacturer obligations — essential requirements, conformity assessment, CE marking — applies to products placed on the market.

Starting evidence collection early matters: the audit trail and version history are most valuable when they cover a product's whole life, not just the weeks before an assessment.
